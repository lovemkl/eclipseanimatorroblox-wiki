## What the Plugin Actually Is

Strip away the launch-week noise and Eclipse Animator Roblox is a serious piece of Studio tooling. Its lineage is explicit: the plugin takes its interaction model from Blender — the full 3D suite whose animation tools have been refined for two decades — and its mission from Moon Animator, the long-dominant community plugin for multi-rig Roblox animation that went paid, leaving a vacuum of wanting. Eclipse Animator's feature set reads like the community's collective wish-list assembled into one tool: an inverse kinematics system you can toggle per-limb, unparenting for rigs whose hierarchies fight the animator, live editing that lets you manipulate keys while the timeline plays, and support for any Roblox animation type the platform supports.

That last phrase deserves emphasis because it is the difference between a toy and a tool. Roblox animation spans several formats and use cases — character animations for rigs, tween-style motion, cutscene choreography — and a plugin that handles "any Roblox animation type" is one an animator can build a workflow around rather than outgrow. The features pages on this wiki cover each capability in depth; the comparison pages set them against Moon Animator's and the built-in editor's, honestly and without cheerleading.

## Who Made It and Why It Exists

The Eclipse Animator Roblox plugin is the work of independent developers in the Roblox creator community, and its existence is best understood as a response to a chain of events the community has chronicled for years. For most of a decade, Moon Animator was the answer to the question "how do I animate in Studio like a real 3D artist?" — until it went paid. The DevForum thread asking for alternatives dates back years and never stopped accumulating replies. Free options existed in fragments: Moon Animator 2's open-source build on GitHub, Studio's built-in Animation Editor, scattered community tools — but nothing positioned as a flagship.

Eclipse Animator Roblox arrived carrying exactly that flag. The launch material was confident — Blender-inspired workflow, IK as a headline, "any animation type" support — and the free-claim pricing model was, depending on who you ask, either a generous gift to the community or the most effective marketing the Roblox plugin ecosystem has ever seen. The result was identical either way: a launch-week stampede, a wave of press and creator coverage, and the moment the word "claimed" entered every animator's vocabulary.

## The Launch Window, Explained

The pricing model deserves its own section because it drove everything. Eclipse Animator Roblox launched free-to-claim for a limited window — approximately three days, per the DevForum announcement and community guides covering the launch. The mechanics, as covered in our claim guide: clicking "Get" on the Creator Store page during the window tied the plugin to your Roblox account permanently. After the window closed, the listing switched to paid-only, and the free-claim path disappeared.

Why structure a launch this way? The generosity reading: the developers rewarded the community that showed up at launch, in the platform's own tradition of early adopters getting permanent perks. The marketing reading: nothing spreads a plugin faster than a deadline, and the DevForum thread urging everyone to claim before the paid switch did the work of an advertising budget. Both readings can be true simultaneously — what matters for this wiki is the aftermath: a user base split between permanent free owners and everyone deciding whether the paid price is worth it, and a plugin whose reputation was made in a single week.

## What Claiming Actually Means

For the people who claimed during the window, the situation is simple and good: Eclipse Animator Roblox is in your inventory, permanently, at the price of nothing. Animators in Studio daily will amortize the cost in a week; hobbyists opening Studio monthly may find the free alternatives sufficient.
