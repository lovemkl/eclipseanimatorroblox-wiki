Eclipse Animator versus Moon Animator is the argument the Roblox animation community has been having since launch week, and it is a real argument because both tools are genuinely good. Moon Animator is the established standard — years of dominance, an ocean of tutorials, the workflow a generation of Studio animators grew up on, now paid. Eclipse Animator is the new challenger — Blender-inspired, IK-first, live-editing, launched through a free-claim stampede. This page compares them on the axes that decide: workflow, features, price and maturity. It ends with a recommendation, because a comparison that refuses to conclude is a brochure.

## The Lineage Matters

Understand first that these tools are related by purpose and separated by philosophy. Both exist because Studio's built-in animation tooling has always been adequate rather than excellent, and both answer the professional question: how should a serious animator work in Roblox Studio? Moon Animator's answer, shipped years ago, was the multi-rig timeline — Studio-flavored, practical, immediately graspable. The Eclipse Animator Roblox plugin's answer, shipped this season, imports the workflow of Blender — the open-source 3D suite whose animation tools set an industry standard — and adapts it to Studio's rigs and conventions. The comparison is therefore partly a comparison of traditions: the homegrown standard against the imported one.

## Workflow: Familiar Against Different

Moon Animator's workflow is the one most working Roblox animators already know: select a rig, key its joints, manage tracks, scrub and refine. Its familiarity is its superpower — a decade of community tutorials, forum answers and muscle memory stand behind every action, and for teams, it is the shared language. Its weakness is the same coin's other face: the workflow is Studio-shaped, built around joint-rotation posing and the conventions that implies, and animators arriving from external 3D tools have always felt the friction of translating their habits into Moon Animator's dialect.

The Eclipse Animator Roblox workflow is the translation layer eliminated. Viewport-direct posing (grab the limb, not the property), the IK toggle for effect-driven motion, live editing during playback — these are Blender's interaction patterns, and they either feel like coming home (if you have them) or like learning a new dialect (if you don't). The launch-week community split predictably along exactly this line: migrants from external tools praised the feel; Moon Animator lifetimes reported an adjustment period followed by conversion or return. The honest summary: Moon Animator optimizes for the animator Studio made; Eclipse Animator optimizes for the animator Blender made; Roblox contains both in large numbers.

## Features: The IK Question

Moon Animator's feature set is mature and broad: multi-rig timelines, robust key management, the cutscene capabilities that made its reputation, and the stability of years of iteration. What it lacks, conspicuously, is the Eclipse Animator Roblox plugin's headline trio: no inverse-kinematics toggle (posing is joint-rotation, always), no live editing during playback (edit, scrub, edit), and no unparenting system for fighting rig hierarchies. These are not small omissions — they are the three features that define the newer tool's identity, and each addresses a daily friction in the older one.

The counterweight is maturity. Moon Animator's years in production have sanded edges the new tool has not yet encountered: odd rig quirks, edge-case stability, the long tail of platform changes a tool survives by adapting to. The Eclipse Animator Roblox plugin is weeks old at this writing — impressive, responsive to its community, but young, and youth is a feature list entry that only time edits. Animators choosing for mission-critical production work weigh this axis heavily, and should.

## Price: The Launch Story

The pricing asymmetry is the season's drama. Moon Animator went paid — the event that created the demand vacuum the new plugin filled. Eclipse Animator launched free-to-claim for roughly three days, then paid-only, splitting the community into permanent free owners and deciders (the claim guide documents the mechanics and aftermath). Comparing prices today: both are paid acquisitions for new users, at different price points the live listings carry. The practical difference is not the sticker but the structure — Moon Animator's paid status is settled; the Eclipse Animator Roblox pricing story just began, and launch-window precedent makes future windows an open question.

And the free third option belongs in any honest price row: Moon Animator 2's open-source build on GitHub, importable into Studio at no cost, missing the newer tool's IK/live-editing/unparenting but carrying the classic multi-rig workflow. For budget-constrained animators, the real comparison may be free-Moon against paid-Eclipse, and the comparison below this one — against the built-in editor — completes the decision space.

## The Recommendation

Reduce it to the decider's situations. **If you animate with external 3D habits** — Blender, Maya, any suite with viewport posing — Eclipse Animator is your tool; the workflow match is worth more than Moon Animator's maturity, and the IK toggle alone justifies it. **If you animate in a Moon Animator team** on shared projects, stay; a workflow standard's value compounds with every teammate who shares it, and switching costs are paid in coordination. **If you are new and choosing fresh**, learn the Eclipse Animator Roblox workflow — the habits it teaches transfer outward to the wider 3D world, while Moon Animator's dialect transfers mainly backward into its own history. **If budget is the axis**, the free Moon Animator 2 build plus the built-in editor covers fundamentals, and upgrading later is frictionless.

The bottom line, stated plainly because launch season deserves plain statements: Moon Animator is the great tool of the last era of Roblox animation; Eclipse Animator is the credible start of the next one. The claim window that separates their pricing stories has a parallel in their capability stories — one closed and settled, one just opened. Animators rarely get to watch an era change with this clarity; whichever side you choose, you are choosing with full information, and that is more than most tool arguments ever offer.
